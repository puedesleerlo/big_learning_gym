# Causality lab: research and teaching notes

Research checked 2026-09-20. This is an original teaching synthesis, not a reproduction of the linked readings. Source metadata below was checked against author, publisher, proceedings, or arXiv pages. The lab should introduce the learner's question before notation, pair each research reading with a prerequisite concept, and distinguish simulation ground truth from real-world causal evidence.

## Learning design

Use a repeating sequence: predict → manipulate a small model → observe → explain → read a short paper bridge → state a limitation. Progress should reflect explanations and solved examples, not merely opening readings. Let the learner inspect later research early while showing which prerequisite lessons will make it understandable.

The proposed 12 lessons are a teaching design, not a claim about an optimal learning sequence. Each can begin with a concrete, non-medical toy system: tutoring and exam scores, thermostat and room temperature, lamps and switches, or robot commands and generated movement. Keep mathematical derivations optional on the first pass.

1. **Three different questions: observe, intervene, imagine.** A model can predict a score from tutoring attendance without identifying what assigning tutoring would change. Ask the learner to sort observational, interventional, and individual counterfactual questions. Bridge: causal-learn's discovery task [S3] versus effect estimation [S1]. Experiment: change assignment policy while holding the outcome mechanism fixed. Check: name the intervention, outcome, target population, and comparison.
2. **Variables, arrows, and mechanisms.** Build a three-node directed acyclic graph and specify how each value is produced. A graph is a hypothesis about mechanisms, not a drawing of correlations. Bridge: representations in Spirtes and Zhang [S2]. Experiment: cut incoming arrows to a switch when forcing its value. Check: changing the mechanism for X leaves other assignments intact under the chosen intervention model.
3. **Confounding and adjustment.** Use prior preparation as a common cause of tutoring and scores. Compare raw and within-preparation differences; then combine strata using the same target-population weights. Foundation: [S1]. Experiment: vary the imbalance in who receives tutoring. Check: explain why exchangeability, positivity, and a well-defined intervention matter; do not mechanically control for every available variable.
4. **Paths, conditional independence, and colliders.** Explore chain, fork, and collider patterns. Conditioning can close a chain/fork but open a collider path; conditioning on a collider's descendant can also open it. Foundations: CMU OLI [S17] and [S2]. Research bridge: selection in sequences [S12]. Experiment: independent skill and luck determine admission; restricting to admitted students can induce association. Check: identify the selection variable and the path that changes.
5. **What observations cannot decide: equivalence.** Different DAGs can imply the same conditional independences. PC returns an equivalence-class representation under its assumptions; an unresolved edge is a scientific result. Sources: [S4], [S18]. Experiment: compare three-node chain and fork models and try an intervention to distinguish them. Check: a CPDAG undirected edge is neither proof of no causation nor a 50/50 posterior.
6. **PC: delete links when evidence supports independence.** Walk through skeleton search, an unshielded collider, then orientation propagation. Use a suitable test for the data; Fisher's Z is not a universal nonlinear independence detector. Sources: [S4], [S18]. Experiment: vary sample size and significance threshold on known synthetic graphs. Check: a large p-value does not prove independence; a per-test alpha does not give graph-wide correctness probability.
7. **Hidden causes and FCI.** Hide a shared driver, then inspect why a causally sufficient method may mislead. Introduce PAG arrowheads, tails, and circles as constraints on an equivalence class of ancestral graphs. Sources: [S5], [S19]. Bridge: the 2025 latent-discovery preprint [S13]. Experiment: compare results with and without the shared driver observed. Check: FCI allows latent causes but does not generally recover their names, exact number, or complete hidden DAG.
8. **Searching and scoring: GES to BOSS.** Explain fit-versus-complexity using candidate graphs, then show why searching variable orders and caching scores can save work. Sources: [S6], [S7]; optional GRaSP [S8]. Experiment: reorder four variables and compare candidate parent sets. Check: distinguish a score, a causal assumption, a finite-sample benchmark, and an asymptotic guarantee. BOSS's published guarantee uses its optional BES phase; benchmark configurations omitted it.
9. **When noise helps: LiNGAM.** Compare regressing Y on X and X on Y; focus on independence of residuals rather than residual size alone. Sources: [S9], [S10]. Experiment: generate X from non-Gaussian noise and Y = 2X + independent noise, then repeat with Gaussian noise. Check: orientation relies on linearity, acyclicity, mutually independent non-Gaussian disturbances, and absence of unobserved confounders in the basic model.
10. **Changing environments as evidence.** Keep a mechanism fixed across two environments but change the input distribution; then change a mechanism too. Source: CD-NOD [S11]. Experiment: move a root variable's distribution and observe downstream shifts. Check: a changing marginal is not automatically a changed mechanism; useful shifts require assumptions about their structure. Pooling data can conceal these distinctions.
11. **Learning the variables themselves.** Pixels and embeddings are measurements; underlying causal variables may be hidden. Introduce invertible mixing, sparsity, and why multiple environments add information. Source: ICML 2024 CRL [S14]. Experiment: mix two hidden sliders into two observed dials; compare one environment with several independently changing environments. Check: recovering a moralized graph or components up to transformations is weaker than identifying every directed causal relation.
12. **Read a frontier claim like a researcher.** Choose FCIT [S13], natural counterfactuals [S15], or CD-LAM [S16], then fill in question, assumptions, method, evaluated evidence, unresolved issue, and proposed test. Compare the primary paper with Aether's essay [S20] or research post [S21]. Experiment: for a toy world model, hold initial state fixed and change only the command; separately vary a visual distraction. Check: realistic predictions, correct responses to tested commands, and validated behavior in deployment are different achievements.

For lesson 12, a concrete critique of CD-LAM is that its debiasing stage uses foreground masks and coarse action labels unavailable to its baseline. An ablation with matched extra supervision helps distinguish why performance improved. This is an evaluation question, not a dismissal of the method. Natural counterfactuals supplies another precise question: allowing upstream changes answers a different counterfactual than a surgical intervention with upstream variables fixed.

## Verified source catalog

### Foundations and implemented methods

- **S1 — Hernán, Miguel A.; Robins, James M. (2020). _Causal Inference: What If_.** Author-hosted, freely downloadable textbook with evolving revisions. Use the [author's book page](https://miguelhernan.org/whatifbook) to obtain the current version. Suitable for defining causal questions, randomized versus observational comparisons, identification assumptions, and adjustment. This is a public supplemental source; it has not been identified as one of the user's two supplied books.
- **S2 — Spirtes, Peter; Zhang, Kun (2016). “Causal discovery and inference: concepts and recent methodological advances.” _Applied Informatics_ 3, article 3.** [Publisher/DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40535-016-0018-x), [open full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4841209/). A broad conceptual bridge from graphical and functional models to discovery; use as orientation, not evidence that every data set identifies a unique causal model.
- **S3 — Zheng, Yujia; Huang, Biwei; Chen, Wei; Ramsey, Joseph; Gong, Mingming; Cai, Ruichu; Shimizu, Shohei; Spirtes, Peter; Zhang, Kun (2024). “Causal-learn: Causal Discovery in Python.” _JMLR_ 25(60):1–8.** [Paper](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v25/23-0970.html), [repository](https://github.com/py-why/causal-learn). Software publication; implementation availability does not itself validate an inferred graph. The official repository points to causallearn.com as its online agentic version.
- **S4 — Kalisch, Markus; Bühlmann, Peter (2007). “Estimating High-Dimensional Directed Acyclic Graphs with the PC-Algorithm.” _JMLR_ 8:613–636.** [Paper](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v8/kalisch07a.html). An analysis of PC for sparse high-dimensional Gaussian DAGs with explicit regularity and faithfulness conditions. The consistency theorem is not a finite-sample guarantee for arbitrary data or arbitrary tests.
- **S5 — Zhang, Jiji (2008). “On the completeness of orientation rules for causal discovery in the presence of latent confounders and selection bias.” _Artificial Intelligence_ 172(16–17):1873–1896.** [Publisher/DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.artint.2008.08.001). Establishes completeness of augmented FCI orientation rules under the stated assumptions. Completeness means extracting the invariant information available from the relevant independence relations, not resolving all possible causal uncertainty.
- **S6 — Chickering, David Maxwell (2002). “Optimal Structure Identification With Greedy Search.” _JMLR_ 3:507–554.** [Paper and erratum](https://jmlr.csail.mit.edu/papers/v3/chickering02b.html). GES searches equivalence classes; asymptotic correctness relies on the paper's graphical and scoring conditions. A score fit to observational data cannot by itself resolve Markov-equivalent directions.
- **S7 — Andrews, Bryan; Ramsey, Joseph; Sanchez Romero, Ruben; Camchong, Jazmin; Kummerfeld, Erich (2023). “Fast Scalable and Accurate Discovery of DAGs Using the Best Order Score Search and Grow Shrink Trees.” _NeurIPS_ 36.** [Proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/c9cde817d04811ba28e44071bd9f76a5-Abstract-Conference.html), [full paper](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/c9cde817d04811ba28e44071bd9f76a5-Paper-Conference.pdf). Greedy permutation search plus cached grow/shrink scores. Proposition 2 gives asymptotic equivalence-class recovery with BES enabled under Markov, faithfulness, IID, and model-family conditions. The reported experiments disable BES. Evaluate speed and graph accuracy separately; do not generalize fMRI benchmarks into biological validation of every discovered edge.
- **S8 — Lam, Wai-Yin; Andrews, Bryan; Ramsey, Joseph (2022). “Greedy relaxations of the sparsest permutation algorithm.” _UAI_, PMLR 180:1052–1062.** [Full paper](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v180/lam22a/lam22a.pdf). GRaSP trades exhaustive permutation enumeration for local operations and studies weaker conditions than standard faithfulness. Useful optional bridge for understanding what an assumption relaxation means.
- **S9 — Shimizu, Shohei; Hoyer, Patrik O.; Hyvärinen, Aapo; Kerminen, Antti (2006). “A Linear Non-Gaussian Acyclic Model for Causal Discovery.” _JMLR_ 7:2003–2030.** [Full paper](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume7/shimizu06a/shimizu06a.pdf). LiNGAM gains directional information from functional and noise constraints; its basic model excludes hidden confounding and feedback.
- **S10 — Shimizu, Shohei; Inazumi, Takanori; Sogawa, Yasuhiro; Hyvärinen, Aapo; Kawahara, Yoshinobu; Washio, Takashi; Hoyer, Patrik O.; Bollen, Kenneth (2011). “DirectLiNGAM: A Direct Method for Learning a Linear Non-Gaussian Structural Equation Model.” _JMLR_ 12:1225–1248.** [Paper](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v12/shimizu11a.html). Distinct author list from the 2006 paper. Its finite number of algorithm steps must not be confused with correctness from a finite noisy data set; the correctness statement assumes the model and infinite sample.
- **S11 — Huang, Biwei; Zhang, Kun; Zhang, Jiji; Ramsey, Joseph; Sanchez-Romero, Ruben; Glymour, Clark; Schölkopf, Bernhard (2020). “Causal Discovery from Heterogeneous/Nonstationary Data.” _JMLR_ 21(89):1–53.** [Paper](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v21/19-232.html), [full text](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume21/19-232/19-232.pdf). CD-NOD uses structured variation across environments/time to discover changing mechanisms and obtain causal information. Independent-change and augmented-graph assumptions are substantive; a distribution shift alone is insufficient.

### Recent research: precise claims and limits

- **S12 — Zheng, Yujia; Tang, Zeyu; Qiu, Yiwen; Schölkopf, Bernhard; Zhang, Kun (2024). “Detecting and Identifying Selection Structure in Sequential Data.” _ICML_, PMLR 235:61498–61525.** [Proceedings](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/zheng24k.html). Studies selection as part of the generating structure, with nonparametric identification under appropriate structural conditions and experiments on synthetic data and music. It does not show that every selected observational sample reveals its selection process.
- **S13 — Ramsey, Joseph; Andrews, Bryan; Spirtes, Peter (2025). “Efficient Latent Variable Causal Discovery: Combining Score Search and Targeted Testing.” arXiv:2510.04263v3, revised 2025-11-05.** [Versioned metadata](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04263v3), [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04263v3). **Preprint.** BOSS-FCI/GRaSP-FCI replace the score-search stage in GFCI; FCIT targets tests; LV-Dumb is explicitly heuristic and lacks strict soundness for latent confounding. Structural validity of a PAG is not proof that it is the correct PAG. The 100-node experiments use path-based comparisons to the true DAG, not full true-PAG comparisons, and cap conditioning-set depth. Check metric definitions before comparing results across experiment sizes. Version 1 had only Ramsey and Andrews; version 3 includes Spirtes.
- **S14 — Zhang, Kun; Xie, Shaoan; Ng, Ignavier; Zheng, Yujia (2024). “Causal Representation Learning from Multiple Distributions: A General Setting.” _ICML_, PMLR 235:60057–60075.** [Proceedings](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/zhang24br.html), [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05052v3). With smooth/invertible representation assumptions, sparsity, sufficiently diverse changes, and the appropriate faithfulness conditions, results connect the recovered latent Markov network to the causal DAG's moralized graph. This is not general exact recovery of a directed latent DAG. Identifiability up to transformations and finite-sample representation quality must be discussed separately.
- **S15 — Hao, Guang-Yuan; Zhang, Jiji; Huang, Biwei; Wang, Hao; Zhang, Kun (2024). “Natural Counterfactuals With Necessary Backtracking.” _NeurIPS_ 37.** [Proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/1b10264c77a2a1e0ef8abfbd68d36583-Abstract-Conference.html), [full paper](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/1b10264c77a2a1e0ef8abfbd68d36583-Paper-Conference.pdf). Proposes an optimization framework that permits controlled changes to upstream variables to improve a chosen notion of naturalness. Teach this after standard abduction–action–prediction. Feasibility depends on the adopted model/criterion; this is not a universal replacement for interventional counterfactuals.
- **S16 — Wei, Yufan; Zhou, Kun; Mao, Lingjun; Zhang, Zijun; Xu, Ziming; Xi, Ziqiao; Liang, Shuang; Han, Ruobing; Yan, Yuchen; Wang, Xinyue; Feng, Fan; Huang, Biwei (2026). “Causally Debiased Latent Action Model for Embodied Action Conditioned World Models.” arXiv:2607.09185v1, submitted 2026-07-10.** [Metadata](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09185v1), [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.09185v1), [code](https://github.com/yufanwei/CD-LAM). **Preprint.** CD-LAM changes latent-action training and reports improved action following and adaptation on 2B/14B world models. Its additional masks/action labels are a concrete experimental consideration. Generated video response tests support the tested setting; they do not establish full physical causal identification or general deployed-robot reliability.

## Content and rubric guardrails

- Keep source claims, independently designed toy experiments, and the learner's proposed hypotheses visibly distinct.
- Define **identification** (what is uniquely determined by data plus assumptions), **estimation** (learning a numerical quantity from samples), and **discovery** (learning graph information). Do not conflate them.
- Require a valid explanation of the causal question, mechanism/graph, assumptions, and uncertainty. A response containing the right keywords is insufficient evidence of mastery.
- A CPDAG arrow is invariant within an equivalence class under assumptions; a PAG circle is endpoint uncertainty, not a probability. PAG directed edges need not represent direct causal links in the full latent-variable DAG.
- Bootstrap graph frequency is stability under the chosen resampling procedure, not posterior probability that an edge is true. Report randomness, sample size, score/test choice, and background constraints.
- Do not silently turn toy linear-Gaussian experiments into demonstrations of LiNGAM's non-Gaussian identification or nonlinear methods.
- No supplied local books were identified by this research subtask. The two books the user mentions must be attached or located before claiming their contents are incorporated. S1 and the books named on the public CMU syllabus are supplemental references, not substitutes presented as the user's uploads.
